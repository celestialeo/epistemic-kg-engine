The nervous system coordinates sensation, movement, and internal regulation by utilizing interacting cells and circuits. The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord, while the peripheral nervous system connects to the rest of the body. Sensory pathways carry information toward the central nervous system, and motor pathways transmit commands toward muscles or glands. Dendrites receive many incoming signals, the cell body supports cellular activity, and axons carry electrical signals toward other cells. Neurons differ in shape and connectivity; their contribution depends on connections and activity within a circuit rather than shape alone.

A resting neuron maintains an electrical potential difference across its membrane and has an uneven ion distribution of its membrane. The membrane allows ions to cross more readily than others. A sodium potassium pump maintains the underlying concentration gradients of the underlying concentration gradients but does not directly generate the rapid rising phase of each action potential. When sufficient depolarization occurs in a typical axon, it opens voltage gated sodium channels in a typical axon. These channels permit sodium entry in a typical axon, which induces further depolarization of the membrane. Sodium channel inactivation helps repolarization of it, and increased potassium conductance also helps repolarization of it. Reaching the threshold enables an action potential via the all_or_none_principle principle. Stronger stimulation can increase firing frequency rather than increasing spike amplitude exclusively. During the absolute refractory period, initiation of an action potential is prevented in that membrane region even with a stronger stimulus.

An action potential in the presynaptic terminal activates voltage gated calcium channels, causing a transition from a closed to an open state that allows calcium entry. This calcium entry promotes the fusion of neurotransmitter containing vesicles with the terminal membrane, resulting in released neurotransmitter. The released neurotransmitter crosses the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic cell. The effect relies on the receptor and the cell's ionic conditions. While a single excitatory input does not necessarily trigger an action potential, excitatory input generally raises the likelihood of firing. Conversely, inhibitory input can reduce the likelihood of firing or triggering an action potential. Signals arriving at different synapses or at closely spaced times can combine to produce a postsynaptic response. Signaling termination is helped by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, and diffusion. The role of reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, and diffusion in terminating signals varies based on the transmitters and synapses involved.

Glial cells support neural signaling in several ways. Astrocytes help regulate the extracellular chemical environment, including potassium levels around active neurons. Oligodendrocytes form myelin around axons in the central nervous system and can myelinate segments of several axons. Schwann cells form myelin in the peripheral nervous system around one axon. Myelin increases electrical insulation between the nodes of Ranvier. Nodes of Ranvier are locations for action potential regeneration. The arrangement permits rapid saltatory conduction without requiring an action potential to be regenerated at every point beneath the myelin, whereas it does not require such regeneration at every point beneath the myelin. An axon is not always a myelinated axon; however, an unmyelinated axon can still conduct signal conduction. Damage to myelin can slow conduction of myelinated axons, reduce the speed of conduction of myelinated axons, and induce conduction failure in conduction failure of myelinated axons.

The spinal withdrawal reflex illustrates a circuit organizing response. A painful stimulus stimulates the firing of sensory neurons whose signals enter the spinal cord. These sensory neurons send signals to the spinal cord. Input can excite motor neurons supplying flexor muscles through spinal interneurons, resulting in a pattern that facilitates the withdrawal of the affected limb. Inhibition of opposing muscle motor neurons contributes to movement. Inhibition does not always mean the whole behavior stops. A spinal response can begin before conscious awareness of the stimulus. Ascending pathways carry information toward the brain, while descending signals from the brain can modify spinal activity. The reflex demonstrates local circuit processing within a nervous system. The nervous system has regions whose regions remain connected and influence one another.
