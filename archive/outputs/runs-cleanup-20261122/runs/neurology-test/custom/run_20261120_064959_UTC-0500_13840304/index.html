The input document

1. The nervous system coordinates sensation, movement, and internal regulation through interacting cells and circuits. The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord, whereas the peripheral nervous system connects these structures with the rest of the body. Sensory pathways carry information toward the central nervous system, and motor pathways carry commands toward muscles or glands. In a typical neuron, dendrites receive many incoming signals, the cell body supports cellular activity, and an axon carries electrical signals toward other cells. This description is a useful pattern, but neurons differ in shape and connectivity. A neuron's contribution therefore depends on its connections and activity within a circuit, rather than on its shape alone.

2. A resting neuron maintains an electrical potential difference across its membrane because ions are distributed unevenly and the membrane allows some ions to cross more readily than others. The sodium-potassium pump helps maintain the underlying concentration gradients; it does not directly generate the rapid rising phase of each action potential. In a typical axon, sufficient depolarization opens voltage-gated sodium channels, allowing sodium to enter and further depolarize the membrane. Sodium-channel inactivation and increased potassium conductance then help repolarize it. Once threshold is reached, an action potential is approximately all-or-none. Stronger stimulation can increase firing frequency rather than simply making every spike larger. During the absolute refractory period, another action potential cannot be initiated in that membrane region, even with a stronger stimulus.

3. At a chemical synapse, an arriving action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels in the presynaptic terminal. Calcium entry promotes the fusion of neurotransmitter-containing vesicles with the terminal membrane. Released neurotransmitter crosses the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic cell. The effect depends on the receptor and the cell's ionic conditions, so a transmitter's name alone does not fully specify its action. Excitatory input generally increases the likelihood of firing, but a single excitatory input does not necessarily trigger an action potential. Inhibitory input can reduce that likelihood. Signals arriving at different synapses or at closely spaced times can combine. Reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, and diffusion help terminate signaling, with their relative roles varying between transmitters and synapses.

4. Glial cells support neural signaling in several ways. Astrocytes help regulate the extracellular chemical environment, including potassium levels around active neurons. Oligodendrocytes form myelin around axons in the central nervous system, whereas Schwann cells form myelin in the peripheral nervous system. One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of several axons; a myelinating Schwann cell forms one segment around one axon. Myelin increases electrical insulation between the nodes of Ranvier, where action potentials are regenerated. This arrangement permits rapid saltatory conduction without requiring an action potential to be regenerated at every point beneath the myelin. Not all axons are myelinated, and unmyelinated axons can still conduct signals. Damage to myelin can slow conduction or cause conduction failure, rather than necessarily destroying the entire neuron.

5. A spinal withdrawal reflex illustrates how a circuit can organize a response. A painful stimulus activates sensory neurons whose signals enter the spinal cord. Through spinal interneurons, this input can excite motor neurons supplying flexor muscles and inhibit motor neurons supplying opposing extensor muscles. The resulting pattern helps withdraw the affected limb. In this circuit, inhibition of an opposing muscle's motor neurons contributes to movement; inhibition does not always mean that the whole behavior stops. The spinal response can begin before conscious awareness of the stimulus, while ascending pathways also carry information toward the brain. Descending signals from the brain can modify spinal activity. The reflex therefore demonstrates local circuit processing within a nervous system whose regions remain connected and influence one another.
