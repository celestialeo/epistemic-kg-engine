Neurons are specialized signaling cells forming neural circuits and networks. Their electrical messages reach neurons, muscles, or glands. Neurons vary in shape and size but have a soma, dendrites, and an axon.

The soma, or cell body, houses the nucleus, most cytoplasm, and machinery producing and transporting essential proteins. Branched dendrites extend from the soma, receiving other neurons' signals at synapses. Axons extend from the soma, carry electrical signals, and may branch before reaching terminals. Axon lengths range from under a centimeter to over a meter. Axon terminals release neurotransmitters, chemical messengers conveying signals across synapses.

Glia support neurons. Earlier estimates placed glia-to-neuron ratios at 10:1 or higher; newer investigations suggest roughly 1:1 in some human and other primate brain regions. Ratios vary considerably across regions.

Four major glial types occur in the central nervous system. Brain astrocyte networks regulate ion concentrations around neurons, supply nutrients, and help control new neuronal connections. Microglia, the brain's principal immune cells, mainly act as phagocytes against infection and cellular damage and also regulate new connections.

Ependymal cells produce cerebrospinal fluid, cushioning the brain within the skull. Oligodendrocytes enhance neuronal function by wrapping axons with fatty myelin sheaths.
