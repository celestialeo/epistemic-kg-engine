The water cycle describes how water moves between the atmosphere, land, and bodies of water. Solar energy enables evaporation from oceans, lakes, and moist ground, causing liquid water to change into water vapor. This vapor then undergoes condensation, which causes it to change back into liquid. Throughout this process, water is stored in various reservoirs including oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, groundwater, snow, and ice.

Plants absorb water through their roots and emit vapor through leaf openings called stomata. The process of transpiration involves leaf openings participating in the emission of this vapor. Evapotranspiration integrates with evaporation from surfaces, specifically combining these processes with transpiration by plants. When air cooling and saturation occur onto small particles, they enable condensation. Clouds contain tiny liquid droplets and ice crystals.

Cloud particles must grow into precipitation before they can fall. Some falling water evaporates before reaching the ground. Rain and snow participate in returning atmospheric water to the surface.

Infiltration describes the entry of water through the ground surface into soil or other porous material. The rate of infiltration is influenced by soil structure, existing moisture, vegetation, and rainfall intensity. Water in soil may absorb water via roots (d81b2e4d) and may participate in percolation.

Infiltrating water participates in reaching the saturated zone, enabling groundwater recharge. Groundwater fills voids in pore spaces and is stored by an aquifer as usable quantities. A spring occurs in groundwater emergence where groundwater emerges at the surface. Underground storage links to surface water, and groundwater maintains flow in a stream during dry periods.

A watershed is a land area that drains surface water toward a common outlet. Within this system, lakes can temporarily store water, and wetlands can also temporarily store water. Snow and ice hold water as well. Liquid water can enter streams or move into the soil.

Paved surfaces often inhibit infiltration and often influence the rate of rapid runoff. Sustained withdrawals greater than replenishment can reduce groundwater storage. Water does not follow one compulsory loop.
