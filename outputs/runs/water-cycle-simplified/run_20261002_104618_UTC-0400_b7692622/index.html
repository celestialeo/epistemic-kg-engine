The input document

1. The water cycle describes how water moves between the atmosphere, the land, and bodies of water. Oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, groundwater, snow, and ice store water in reservoirs. Evaporation changes liquid water into water vapor, while condensation changes vapor back into liquid. Solar energy supplies much of the energy needed for evaporation from oceans, lakes, and moist ground. The rate also depends on humidity, wind, and the availability of water. Plants absorb water through their roots and release some of it as vapor through leaf openings called stomata. This release is transpiration. Evapotranspiration combines evaporation from surfaces with transpiration by plants. Moving air transports water vapor. When air cools sufficiently and becomes saturated, vapor can condense onto small particles to form droplets. Clouds contain tiny liquid droplets, ice crystals, or both; they are not simply invisible water vapor. Cloud particles must grow before they can fall as precipitation, and some falling water evaporates before reaching the ground. Rain and snow return atmospheric water to the surface.

2. After precipitation reaches land, water can follow several pathways. Some remains on leaves and later evaporates, some flows over the surface, and some enters the ground. Infiltration is the entry of water through the ground surface into soil or other porous material. Soil structure, existing moisture, vegetation, and rainfall intensity affect how quickly infiltration occurs. Water in soil may be absorbed by roots, remain in pore spaces, or move deeper through the subsurface. This deeper movement is called percolation. Water reaching the saturated zone can contribute to groundwater recharge, but not all infiltrating water reaches that zone. Groundwater fills pores or fractures in saturated material. An aquifer is a geologic formation that stores and transmits usable quantities of groundwater. Groundwater can move through connected openings and discharge into streams, lakes, or springs. A spring occurs where groundwater emerges at the surface. These connections allow groundwater to maintain flow in some streams during dry periods, linking underground storage to visible surface water.

3. Surface runoff develops when water flows over the land, including when rainfall exceeds the ground's ability to absorb it or when the ground is already saturated. Gravity moves runoff downslope toward channels, streams, and rivers. A watershed is the land area that drains surface water toward a common outlet. Rivers carry water downstream, while lakes and wetlands can temporarily store it and influence the timing of its movement. Snow and ice also store water; melting releases liquid water that can enter streams or soil. Storage and movement occur over different timescales, so water does not follow one compulsory loop. A water budget compares inputs, outputs, and changes in storage within a defined area and time interval. Human activities can alter this balance. Paved surfaces often reduce infiltration and increase rapid runoff. Pumping removes groundwater, and sustained withdrawals greater than replenishment can reduce groundwater storage. Understanding these pathways helps explain how rainfall, underground reserves, and downstream water availability are connected.
