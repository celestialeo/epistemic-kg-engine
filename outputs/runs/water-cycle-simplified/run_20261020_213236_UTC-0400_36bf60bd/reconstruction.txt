The water cycle describes how water moves between the atmosphere, land, and bodies of water. Solar energy supplies much of the energy for evaporation from oceans, lakes, and moist ground. Oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, groundwater, snow, and ice all store water in reservoirs.

Plants absorb water via their roots and release vapor through leaf openings called stomata. When air cools sufficiently and becomes saturated, it enables vapor to condense onto small particles to form droplets. Clouds contain tiny liquid droplets and ice crystals.

Cloud particles must grow into precipitation before they can fall, while falling water evaporates before reaching the ground.

Infiltration describes the entry of water through the ground surface into soil or other porous material. The speed of infiltration is influenced by soil structure, existing moisture, vegetation, and rainfall intensity. Water in soil may absorb water via roots, store itself in pore spaces, and participate in percolation.

An aquifer stores groundwater, and water reaching the saturated zone enables groundwater recharge. Groundwater also enables flow in some streams during dry periods.

Surface runoff describes water flows over the land when rainfall exceeds the ground's ability to absorb it or when the ground is already saturated. Lakes can temporarily store water (f247c5d1), and wetlands can also temporarily store water (f247c5d1). Snow and ice stores water (d5c878ab). Melting emits liquid water (3d513e36), which can enter streams. Lakes can influence the timing of movement, and wetlands can also influence the timing of movement.

A water budget describes outputs and changes in storage within a defined area and time interval. Human activities can act on balance. Paved surfaces often inhibit infiltration and often cause rapid runoff.
